Last year, if your website came up first for "pool service near me," you got the
call. The link between ranking and getting found is coming apart, and it is
worth ten minutes of your time to understand why.

Here is the change in one number. Google's AI Overview — the block of
AI-written text sitting above the blue links — names its sources. Only **38% of
those citations now come from pages that rank in the top ten organic results**.
A year earlier it was 76%. Ranking first still helps. It no longer decides who
gets named in the answer.

That matters because more customers stop at the answer. They read the two or
three businesses the AI named, they call one, and they never scroll.

## The old scoreboard measured the wrong thing

For twenty years, search was mostly about links. Get other websites to link to
yours, climb the rankings, get the traffic. Entire agencies were built on that
one idea.

The systems writing AI answers appear to weigh things differently. In analyses
of what gets cited, *unlinked* brand mentions — someone writing your business
name in a sentence, with no link attached — correlate with being cited at
0.664. Backlinks correlate at 0.218.

Read that carefully, because it is easy to oversell. Correlation is not proof
of cause, and nobody outside those companies can see how the systems actually
work. But the direction is consistent enough to plan around: being talked about
across the web now matters more than being linked to.

For a local service business, that is good news. You are never going to
outspend a national franchise on link building. You can absolutely get
mentioned more often in your own town.

## The engines do not read the same internet

They are not one thing, and treating them as one thing is where most advice
goes wrong.

- **ChatGPT** leans heavily on Wikipedia and mirrors Bing's index. If Bing
  cannot find you, ChatGPT usually cannot either.
- **Perplexity** leans on Reddit and crawls closer to real time. A forum thread
  from last week can show up in an answer.
- **Claude** is the most conservative of the three and favors established,
  long-standing sources.

Treat that as directional, not gospel. The percentages behind those patterns
come from analyses published by companies that sell AI-visibility tools, and
none of them disclose their methodology. That is the honest version.

The practical takeaway holds either way: there is no single "AI ranking" to
chase. Showing up in three different places, in three different ways, is the
whole game.

## The thing you should not buy

You may get an email offering to add an `llms.txt` file to your website — a
plain-text file that tells AI models what your business does. It gets sold as
the AI version of a sitemap.

About 8.7% of the top 1,000 websites have added one. There is no evidence that
any major AI crawler actually parses it. An analysis of 500 sites found zero
correlation between having the file and getting cited.

The file is harmless, and it costs about ten minutes to write. Paying someone a
monthly fee for it as a ranking lever is not harmless. If a vendor leads their
pitch with `llms.txt`, ask what else they are doing and listen closely to the
answer.

## What actually moves the needle

**Get mentioned where the engines already read.** Local news, your chamber or
county business directory, supplier and manufacturer "find a pro" pages, trade
association listings, real review profiles, and the community forums where
people ask for recommendations. One mention in the local paper is worth more
than a hundred links from directories nobody reads.

**Answer real customer questions on your own site, in plain language.** Not
keyword pages. The questions you actually get on the phone: what it costs to
fix a green pool, how often a lawn needs fertilizing in August, what happens if
it rains on service day. Write the answer the way you would say it out loud. AI
answers are assembled out of text that already reads like an answer.

**Keep your name, address, and phone number identical everywhere.** Same
spelling, same suite number, same phone format — on your site, your Google
Business Profile, Yelp, Facebook, and every directory you are listed in.
Machines match a business across sources by those strings. "Street" in one
place and "St." in another quietly costs you.

**Check that you are not blocking the crawlers.** This one catches people. A
line in robots.txt, a security setting at Cloudflare, or a bot-protection
toggle can block GPTBot, PerplexityBot, or ClaudeBot without anyone noticing.
Your site loads fine for customers and is invisible to the engines. It takes
five minutes to check and it is the easiest thing on this list to fix.

**Then measure it.** Type the questions your customers would ask into ChatGPT,
Perplexity, and Google AI Mode. Write down who gets named. Do it again next
month. That is the only scoreboard that reflects what your customers are
actually seeing.

## What this does not mean

It does not mean SEO is dead. The same clean site and the same useful content
help in both places. It does not mean you need to post somewhere every day. And
it does not mean you should panic.

Most local service businesses in most towns are doing none of this yet. The bar
is low right now, which is exactly why it is worth stepping over. The companies
getting named in AI answers three years from now will be the ones that were
being talked about, consistently and locally, starting about now.
